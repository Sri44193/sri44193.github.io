Chapter 8: Dataset Engineering

Building the data that trains and finetunes models: what good data looks like, how much you need, how to collect and synthesise it, how to check it, and how to clean and format it. Much of it doubles as a guide to building great test data.

The big picture

The whole chapter on one page. Come back here after each topic.

📌 The chapter in one sentence A model is only as good as its data. Good datasets balance quality, coverage and quantity; when real data runs short, you can synthesise more (with rules, simulation or AI), but every piece must be verified, deduplicated, cleaned and correctly formatted. The book warns that data is mostly "toil, tears, and sweat".
🧪 Why this chapter is a QA chapter in disguise Training data and test data are built the same way: guidelines, coverage of real usage, edge cases, synthetic variants, deduplication, validation. The book even points out that synthetic data started in software testing (libraries like Faker).

A data-centric view of AI

Improving the data instead of the model 📖 PDF p. 387–389

🧠 Model-centric AI

Better architectures, bigger models, new training tricks. Classic benchmark: same dataset (e.g. ImageNet), who builds the best model?

📚 Data-centric AI

Better data. Newer benchmarks: same model, who builds the best dataset? E.g. Andrew Ng's 2021 competition, DataComp (judged on 38 downstream tasks).

🧩 How fast data work has grown GPT-3's paper credited 2 people with data collection and cleaning. Three years later, GPT-4 credited 80 (not counting contracted annotators). Data labellers, dataset creators and data quality engineers are now dedicated roles.
🧪 Testing angle "Data quality engineer" is a natural next step for a QA lead in AI teams: the same skills of defining quality, building checks and finding edge cases, applied to datasets.

What data you need

The data must show the behaviour you want 📖 PDF p. 389–391

Training typeData format
Self-supervised finetuningPlain sequences of text
Instruction finetuning (SFT)(instruction, response)
Preference finetuning(instruction, winning response, losing response)
Reward modelPreference format, or ((instruction, response), score)

🧠 Chain-of-thought data

To teach step-by-step answers, the data must contain step-by-step answers. Adding them nearly doubled accuracy on some tasks. But they're slow to write ("23 apples − 20 = 3, + 6 = 9" vs just "9"), so they're rare.

🛠️ Tool-use data

Experts forget steps when explaining; you often need to watch them work. And what's efficient for people (clicking through a website) isn't for AI (calling an API), so teams often simulate tool use. Tool data may need special multi-message formats.

💬 Single vs multi-turn

Single-turn teaches answering one instruction (easier to get). Multi-turn teaches solving tasks with clarifications and corrections (harder to collect).

🧩 Curation also means removing data (the book's example) Users complain the chatbot is arrogant: asked to fact-check a statement, it says it's correct, then rewrites it unasked. The cause: training examples containing unsolicited suggestions. Fix: remove them and add examples of fact-checking without rewriting.
🎯 The book's analogy: cooking Quality = fresh ingredients (spoiled ones ruin the dish) · coverage = the right mix (not too much sugar) · quantity = how much of everything.
3

Data quality

A little good data beats a lot of noisy data 📖 PDF p. 392–393

🧩 Evidence
  • The Yi team found 10K carefully crafted instructions beat hundreds of thousands of noisy ones.
  • LIMA: a 65B Llama finetuned on just 1,000 curated examples gave answers equal to or preferred over GPT-4's in 43% of cases, though it was less robust than production models.
  • Llama 3's team found human data more error-prone than expected for nuanced safety rules, so they built AI-assisted annotation tools.

✅ Six signs of high-quality data

🎯 Relevant

19th-century law is irrelevant for today's legal questions, unless the task is about 19th-century law.

📐 Aligned with the task

Factual tasks need correct answers; creative tasks creative ones; concise tasks concise ones; "score and justify" needs both.

🤝 Consistent

Two annotators on the same example should agree; two essays scored 3 should be similar. A good guideline is key.

🧹 Correctly formatted

No stray HTML tags, trailing spaces, odd newlines, mixed casing or number formats.

🔂 Sufficiently unique

Duplicates add bias and can contaminate test sets.

⚖️ Compliant

Follows internal policies and laws, e.g. no personal data if that's not allowed.

⚠️ A bug the author still remembers A column of decimal numbers was accidentally stored as integers, silently rounding every value and causing baffling behaviour. Formatting errors are quiet and expensive.
🧪 Testing angle These six characteristics are a data validation checklist, and they apply equally to your evaluation sets. Write automated checks for format, duplicates and PII, and measure annotator agreement for consistency.
4

Data coverage (diversity)

Cover the range of real usage 📖 PDF p. 393–396

🧒 Plain English The data should cover the problems users actually bring, and the ways they phrase them: short and detailed instructions, typos if users make typos, every programming language your users care about. Which kinds of diversity matter depends on the app: a French-to-English translator needs varied topics and styles, not varied languages; a global shopping bot needs linguistic and cultural variety.

🍰 Llama 3's data mix changes by training phase

Math, reasoning and code make up nearly half of pre-training and SFT data, far more than on the web, because high-quality code and math boost reasoning. Preference data uses much less, to mirror what users really ask. Meta credited Llama 3's gains mainly to data quality and diversity, not architecture.

🧩 Quality AND diversity (Zhou et al., 2023) Three 2,000-example datasets: high-quality but narrow · diverse but low-quality · both. The model trained on the one with both did best. One study even found that adding more mixed data can make things worse.
📌 Choosing a mix Simplest: mirror real usage. More rigorous: train small models on candidate mixes and extrapolate, as Meta did.
🧪 Testing angle Exactly how you'd design test coverage: list the dimensions that matter (intent, length, language, format, typos, user type) and make sure each is represented, with production-like proportions in at least one set.
5

Data quantity

How much is enough? 📖 PDF p. 396–401

🧒 Plain English Asking how much data you need is like asking how much money you need. One experiment showed a model learning from a single example; some teams finetune on millions. It depends on three things:

🔧 Technique

Full finetuning: tens of thousands to millions of examples. PEFT/LoRA: hundreds to a few thousand.

🧩 Task complexity

Review sentiment needs far less than Q&A on financial filings.

🤖 Base model

The closer it already is, the less you need. With 100 examples, more advanced models finetuned much better; with 550,000, all five models ended up similar.

📌 Practical rules from the book
  • Start with ~50 well-crafted examples. You should usually see improvement after 50–100. No improvement? A bigger dataset rarely helps, but first rule out bad hyperparameters, data or prompts.
  • Little data → PEFT on a stronger model. Lots of data → full finetuning on a smaller model.
  • Too much pre-training can ossify a model so it adapts poorly (smaller models are more prone).
  • Budget caps quantity: $10,000 at $2 per annotation = 5,000 examples, and money spent on data isn't spent on compute.

📉 Diminishing returns (the book's illustration)

First 1,000 examples
+10 points
Next 1,000
+5 points

Finetune on 25%, 50% and 100% of your data and plot the curve: steep means more data will pay off; flat means it won't. Task variety matters too: performance jumped going from 9 to 282 training tasks, then flattened up to 1,836.

🧩 Stretching scarce data: train on cheaper data first
  • Unlabelled → labelled: raw legal documents, then legal Q&A pairs.
  • Less relevant → relevant: tweet sentiment, then product-review sentiment.
  • Synthetic → real: AI-generated medical reports, then the few real ones (harder to get right).

Data acquisition and annotation

Mix and match sources; guidelines are the hard part 📖 PDF p. 401–404

🧒 Plain English The best data usually comes from your own application: it matches your real usage perfectly. A data flywheel uses users' content, usage and feedback to keep improving the product (every AI startup pitch mentions one, the author jokes). Before building your own, check what already exists.

📌 Where to find public data Hugging Face and Kaggle (hundreds of thousands of datasets) · Google Dataset Search · government portals (data.gov, data.gov.in) · ICPSR · UCI and OpenML · Open Data Network · AWS Open Data · framework datasets · evaluation harnesses (EleutherAI's 400+ benchmark datasets, ~2,000 examples each). Always check the licence and where the data came from, and never trust it without inspecting it.
⚠️ The real bottleneck: annotation guidelines LinkedIn and others found writing guidelines among the hardest parts of the whole pipeline. Teams often give up on careful annotation halfway and hope the model figures it out, which is risky. Good news: training guidelines and evaluation guidelines are the same (Ch 4), so investing in evaluation pays twice.

Why synthesise data?

Augmentation, synthesis, and five reasons to use them 📖 PDF p. 404–406

🔁 Data augmentation

New data derived from real data, e.g. flipping a real cat photo.

🧪 Data synthesis

Data that mimics real data, e.g. simulating how a bot's mouse moves. The book uses "synthesis" for both.

🎯 You already know this Generating fake names, addresses and phone numbers with Faker to test an address parser across countries is data synthesis. AI now lets you synthesise far richer things: doctors' notes, contracts, financial statements.
ReasonExample
📈 QuantityRare situations: unusual weather, deep-sea exploration, self-driving accidents
🗺️ CoverageVery short or very long texts, toxic examples for a toxicity detector, rare classes, adversarial examples. Anthropic generated datasets to test 154 behaviours; AI-written sets came close to human ones, sometimes better
💎 QualityTool-use steps AI does better than people; harder maths problems; more consistent preference ratings
🔒 PrivacySynthetic patient records or insurance claims instead of real, sensitive ones
⚗️ DistillationTrain a small model on a big model's outputs (topic 11)
🧪 Testing angle Every one of these reasons applies to test data too, especially coverage (rare and adversarial cases) and privacy (no real customer data in test environments).
8

Traditional synthesis: rules, transformations, simulation

Techniques from testing, gaming and robotics 📖 PDF p. 407–410

📝 Templates

A transaction template (ID, date, amount, merchant, status…) filled by Faker lets fraud models prove themselves before touching real data. Templates also make invoices, CVs, tax forms and maths problems. DeepMind's AlphaGeometry trained on 100 million synthetic examples.

🔀 Transformations

Rotate, crop or scale images; swap words for synonyms ("She's a fantastic nurse" → "She's a great nurse").

🎮 Simulation

Test a self-driving car meeting a horse on the highway virtually, not for real. Robots try many joint movements in simulation. Good for rare events: bankruptcies, material defects, extreme weather. (Sim2Real = making simulation-trained skills work in reality.)

⚖️ Augmenting to reduce bias (the book's examples)

OriginalAugmented
She's a fantastic nurse.He's a fantastic nurse. · She's a fantastic doctor.
The CEO of the firm, Mr. Alex Wang…The CEO of the firm, Ms. Alexa Wang…
Today, my mom made a casserole for dinner.Today, my dad made a casserole for dinner.
Emily has always loved the violin.Mohammed has always loved the violin.
🧩 Perturbation: tiny changes, big failures Changing one pixel fooled models on 67.97% of CIFAR-10 test images and 16.04% of ImageNet ones. An attacker might use this to pass as an authorised employee. Training on perturbed data (noise, brightness, "snow") makes models more robust; BERT's training randomly replaced 1.5% of tokens.
🧪 Testing angle The bias table is a ready-made counterfactual test: swap gender, names or roles in your test inputs and check the AI's output doesn't change unfairly. Perturbation is your robustness test (Ch 5, topic 1).
9

AI-powered data synthesis

Using models to make training data 📖 PDF p. 410–415

🎭 Simulate

AI can simulate API responses without calling the API, or simulate people. OpenAI's Dota 2 bot played ~180 years of games a day against itself (self-play); AlphaGo did the same with Go. One AI can play an upset customer while another plays the support agent.

✏️ Paraphrase

"How to reset my password?" → "I forgot my password." · "How can I change my password?" · "Steps to reset passwords." MetaMath rewrote 15,000 maths problems into ~400,000, and beat bigger models.

🌍 Translate

Into low-resource languages (e.g. Quechua, Lao), checked by back-translation: translate back and compare with the original. Also code to other programming languages.

⚖️ Preference data

An AI judge picks the better answer. NVIDIA asked twice with the order swapped and kept only consistent verdicts, to cancel first-position bias.

🧾 Synthesising instruction data

🧩 Two famous recipes UltraChat: ChatGPT wrote 30 everyday topics → 30–50 subtopics each → instructions and responses. Alpaca: 175 human-written seed examples → GPT-3 generated 52,000 similar pairs.

📌 Remember Synthetic data is used far more in post-training than pre-training: AI can reformat existing knowledge but struggles to create new knowledge. Long-context data can be synthesised too: write Q&A from short chunks, then train with the whole long document as context.
🧪 Testing angle The paraphrase and self-play ideas are great for test generation: have AI paraphrase each golden test question five ways, or play a difficult customer against your bot. Then verify the generated cases (next topic) before trusting them.

Verifying data

People synthesise what they can check 📖 PDF p. 415–417

🧒 Plain English Just as companies build apps they can evaluate (evaluation-driven development), they synthesise data they can verify. That's why so much synthetic data is code: running it proves it works. For everything else, use the evaluation methods from Chapters 3–4.

▶️ Functional checks

Run the code; execute the SQL; back-translate.

🧑‍⚖️ AI verifiers

Score each example 1–5 or good/bad against written quality requirements; filter likely hallucinations with factual-consistency checks.

🕵️ Creative checks

If a detector can easily tell synthetic data from real, it isn't realistic enough. Topic classifiers drop off-topic examples; anomaly detection flags outliers.

📏 Heuristics

Self-Instruct removed: repetitive examples · too-long or too-short instructions · same instruction with different answers · outputs that just repeat the input.

📌 The ultimate test Does the data actually improve the model? For many models, synthetic data has passed that test.

Limits of AI-generated data, and model distillation

Why synthetic data won't fully replace human data 📖 PDF p. 417–420

🗑️ Quality control

Garbage in, garbage out. Without reliable checks, teams hesitate to use it.

🎭 Superficial imitation

Students copy the teacher's style but not its accuracy. Training a weak model on a strong model's maths solutions teaches it to produce things that look like solutions: it learns to hallucinate.

🌀 Model collapse

Training on AI output, generation after generation, can make models forget rare cases (AI over-produces the likely, e.g. "no cancer", and under-produces the rare). Mixing in real data seems to prevent it; no one knows the safe ratio.

🔍 Hidden lineage

If the generator was trained on copyrighted data or on your benchmark, your model inherits copyright risk or a contaminated score.

🧩 Yet it often works Synthetic maths data was nearly as good as real for Llama 2-7B, with no saturation up to ~1 million examples. NVIDIA's Nemotron-4 340B used 98% synthetic data in finetuning. Llama 3's team found training on unverified self-generated data didn't help, but verified self-generated data did.

⚗️ Model distillation

🧒 Plain English A small student model learns to imitate a big teacher model's outputs.
DistilBERT size
60% of BERT

97%
Alpaca vs teacher

DistilBERT is also 60% faster. Alpaca (7B) behaves much like its 175B teacher at about 4% of the size. BuzzFeed cut inference cost by 80% with a LoRA-finetuned model trained on another model's outputs (how well it performed was unclear).

⚠️ Check the licence Many model licences forbid using outputs to train other (especially competing) models. Also, not all synthetic training is distillation: Nemotron-4 340B (the student) outperformed its smaller teacher, Mixtral.

📌 Inspect by hand The author says 15 minutes of staring at data almost always saves hours. As OpenAI's Greg Brockman put it, manual inspection has "probably the highest value-to-prestige ratio" in machine learning. Annotate a few examples yourself and compare; fact-check answers; look for same question with different answers.
🧩 Why duplicates hurt (the book's toy example)
ProductPrice
pencil, red$20
compass, green$2
pencil, red (duplicate)$20
pencil, red (duplicate)$20
pencil, green$1
The repeats may teach "red means expensive". In an Anthropic study, repeating just 0.1% of data 100 times made an 800M model perform like a 400M one. Duplicates split across train and test also contaminate the test set.

🧹 Cleaning wins

Databricks removed stray Markdown and HTML: accuracy +20%, input length −60%.

😴 A hidden quality signal

Annotations from the second half of a labelling session were lower quality, likely due to fatigue.

🧩 Formatting: training format = usage format A 3-shot prompt ("Item: burger / Label: edible …") can become training rows like burger --> edible. After finetuning, the prompt is just {INPUT} -->, which is much shorter. But use exactly that format: "burger" (no arrow), "Item: burger -->" (extra prefix) or "burger --> " (extra space) can all cause problems.
🧪 Testing angle Build these into your test-data pipeline: a dedupe check between training and test sets (contamination), a PII scan, and tests that production prompts exactly match the finetuning format, including whitespace.

✅ One-page cheat sheet

  1. 📚 Data-centric AI: improve the data, not just the model. Data work is now dedicated roles.
  2. 🧾 Data must show the target behaviour (step-by-step reasoning, tool use, multi-turn). Removing bad examples is curation too.
  3. 💎 Quality beats quantity (LIMA: 1,000 examples). Six signs: relevant, aligned, consistent, well formatted, unique, compliant.
  4. 🗺️ Coverage: match real users' tasks and phrasing. Llama 3 credited gains to data quality and diversity; mixes differ by training phase.
  5. 🔢 Quantity depends on technique, task and base model. Start with ~50 examples; expect diminishing returns; plot the curve.
  6. 🧲 Best data = your own app's (data flywheel). Check public datasets and licences. Guidelines are the bottleneck, and they double as evaluation guidelines.
  7. 🧪 Synthesise for quantity, coverage, quality, privacy, distillation. Roots: Faker-style test data.
  8. 🧰 Traditional: templates, transformations (incl. counterfactual bias swaps), perturbation, simulation.
  9. 🤖 AI-powered: simulate, self-play, paraphrase, translate (+ back-translation), reverse instruction, verified pipelines (Llama 3: 2.7M coding examples).
  10. ✅ Verify: run it, AI verifiers, detectors, heuristics. Synthesise what you can check.
  11. ⚠️ Limits: weak quality control, superficial imitation, model collapse, hidden lineage. Mix in real data. Distillation = small student copies big teacher (licences permitting).
  12. 🧹 Process: inspect by hand → deduplicate → clean and filter → format exactly as used later.

Try it: a data-QA mini lab (optional)

Apply the chapter to your own evaluation set

  1. Run the six quality checks on your golden dataset: relevant? aligned? consistent? formatted? unique? compliant (no real PII)?
  2. Ask a colleague to label 10 of your cases with your rubric. Where do you disagree? Update the guideline.
  3. Use AI to paraphrase 5 cases three ways each. Verify each new case by hand before adding it.
  4. Write 5 counterfactual cases (swap names, gender or role). Does your feature's output change when it shouldn't?
  5. Check for duplicates between any examples you used to write prompts and your test set.
