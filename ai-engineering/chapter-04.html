Evaluation-driven development

Define how you'll judge success before building 📖 PDF p. 184–185

🎯 Analogy: test-driven development In TDD you write the tests before the code. In evaluation-driven development you write the evaluation criteria before building the AI feature.
🧒 Plain English The AI apps that make it to production are mostly the ones whose success is easy to measure: recommendations (more purchases), fraud detection (money saved), code (does it run?), classification. The book warns this is like looking for lost keys under the streetlight: easier, but valuable apps may be hiding where measurement is hard. It calls evaluation the biggest bottleneck to AI adoption.

🧺 Four buckets of criteria (example: summarising a legal contract)

⚖️

Domain capability

Does the model understand legal contracts?

✍️

Generation quality

Is the summary coherent and faithful to the contract?

📏

Instruction following

Does it respect the requested format and length?

💰

Cost and latency

How much does each summary cost, and how long does it take?

🧪 Testing angle This is a test strategy template. For any AI feature, write at least one measurable criterion in each bucket before development starts, and agree them with product, like acceptance criteria on a story.
2

Domain-specific capability

Does the model know the subject at all? 📖 PDF p. 185–187

🧒 Plain English A model that never saw Latin can't translate Latin. Domain skill is usually checked with exact methods: running code, or multiple-choice questions (MCQs). In April 2024, 75% of the tasks in EleutherAI's popular evaluation harness were multiple choice (MMLU, AGIEval, ARC-C).

✅ Why MCQs are popular

Easy to create, check and reproduce, with a clear random baseline: 4 options, 1 correct → 25% by guessing. Classification is a special case where every question has the same options.

⚠️ Why MCQs aren't enough

Picking the right answer is classification, not generation. Good for knowledge and reasoning, poor for summaries, translation or essays. And answers can change with an extra space or the word "Choices:".

🧩 Beyond "does the code run?" A correct SQL query that takes too long or uses too much memory may be unusable, like a car that runs but burns too much fuel. BIRD-SQL also scores efficiency (runtime vs the reference query). Code readability has no exact metric, so teams use AI judges.
🧪 Testing angle The formatting sensitivity is itself a test: run your MCQ or classification cases with small presentation changes (spacing, option labels, "Choices:") and check the answers don't change.
3

Generation quality and factual consistency

Detecting made-up facts 📖 PDF p. 187–194

🧒 Plain English Old text-generation metrics were fluency (sounds natural?) and coherence (well structured?). Modern models are fluent, so those matter less. The new worries are factual consistency (hallucination) and safety.

📄 Local factual consistency

Checked against given context. A summary must match its document; a support bot must match company policy. Easier to check.

🌍 Global factual consistency

Checked against general knowledge. Needed for general chatbots and fact-checking. Harder: first you must find reliable sources.

⚠️ The hardest part is deciding what the facts are Is "Messi is the best player in the world" a fact? The web is full of misinformation, and the absence-of-evidence fallacy ("I found no link, so there is none") is easy to fall into. One study found models judge sources by relevance, largely ignoring signals people value, like references or a neutral tone.
🧩 Where models hallucinate most (the author's experience)
  • Niche knowledge: more hallucinations about the Vietnamese Mathematical Olympiad than the International one.
  • Things that don't exist: "What did X say about Y?" when X never said anything about Y.
Lesson: focus your hallucination tests on these kinds of queries.

Safety

Harmful, toxic and biased output 📖 PDF p. 194–196

🤬 Inappropriate language

Profanity, explicit content.

💣 Harmful advice

"How to rob a bank", encouraging self-harm.

😡 Hate speech

Racist, sexist, homophobic or other discriminatory content.

🔪 Violence

Threats, graphic detail.

🧑‍⚕️ Stereotypes

Always female names for nurses, male names for CEOs.

🗳️ Ideological bias

Studies place GPT-4 as more left-libertarian and Llama as more authoritarian.

📌 Tools and benchmarks
  • General AI judges (GPT, Claude, Gemini) can flag many harms if prompted well. Providers also expose moderation tools.
  • Specialised detectors built for human-written toxicity are smaller, faster and cheaper: Perspective API, Facebook's hate-speech model, language-specific classifiers.
  • Benchmarks: RealToxicityPrompts (100,000 prompts likely to trigger toxic completions), BOLD (bias in open-ended generation).
🧪 Testing angle Include a safety slice in every release suite: prompts that invite toxic completions, stereotype probes (e.g. "write a story about a nurse and a CEO") and controversial topics. Use a cheap classifier on everything and an AI judge or human on a sample.

Instruction following, and roleplaying

Does it do what it was told, in the way it was told? 📖 PDF p. 196–201

🧩 Knowing vs obeying You ask for sentiment as NEGATIVE, POSITIVE or NEUTRAL. The model clearly understands the tweets but answers HAPPY and ANGRY. It has the domain skill but weak instruction following, and the app downstream breaks.

📋 IFEval (Google)

25 kinds of instructions a program can check automatically: include keyword X, at most N words, exactly N bullet points, a title in << >>, whole answer in JSON… Score = share of instructions followed.

✅ INFOBench

Broader: content, language and style rules too. Each instruction gets yes/no checks, e.g. "Make a hotel-review questionnaire": Is it a questionnaire? For hotel guests? Helpful for writing reviews? Score = criteria met ÷ total. GPT-4 checked these more accurately than crowd workers, though less accurately than experts.

⚠️ Common trap A bad result can mean a bad model or a bad instruction. If a model fails to write a Vietnamese lục bát poem, maybe it doesn't know the form, or maybe it didn't understand the request. Public benchmarks also won't contain your instructions: if you need YAML, or never want "As a language model…", test exactly that.

🎭 Roleplaying

The 8th most common use in one analysis of a million chatbot conversations. Used for game characters, companions, and as a prompting trick (Ch 5). Check two things:

🗣️ Style

Does it talk like the character? (A quiet character → check average reply length.)

🧠 Knowledge, including what it shouldn't know

A game character must not reveal spoilers; a character who doesn't speak Vietnamese shouldn't answer in Vietnamese.

🧪 Testing angle IFEval-style checks are easy, deterministic tests you can write today: word limits, required keywords, banned phrases, bullet counts, JSON validity. Write one validator per instruction in your system prompt.

Cost and latency

Good answers that are too slow or too pricey are still failures 📖 PDF p. 201–203

🧒 Plain English Balancing several goals at once is called Pareto optimisation. Decide what you can't compromise on, filter out models that fail it, then pick the best of the rest. Many companies choose a lower-quality model because it's cheaper and faster.

⏱️ Latency

Time to first token, time per token, time between tokens, time per query. More output tokens = more latency, so instruct conciseness and set stop conditions. Separate must-have from nice-to-have: nobody refuses lower latency, but high latency is often an annoyance, not a deal breaker.

💰 Cost

APIs charge per token, and cost per token stays roughly flat as you grow. Self-hosting costs compute: it gets cheaper per token at scale. GPUs come with 16/24/48/80 GB, which is why so many models are sized to fit (e.g. 7B, 65B).

The model selection workflow

Not "the best model", but the best model for you 📖 PDF p. 203–205

🧱 Hard attributes

Things you can't (or won't) change: licence, training data, model size, your privacy and control policies.

🧽 Soft attributes

Things you can improve: accuracy, toxicity, factual consistency. The author once saw accuracy jump from 20% to 70% by splitting a task into two steps, and once gave up on a model after weeks of tweaking.

The same attribute can be either: latency is soft if you host the model and can optimise it, hard if someone else hosts it.

Open source, open weight, and licences

What "open" really means for models 📖 PDF p. 205–207

TermWhat you get
Open weightThe trained model to download, but not its training data. Most "open source" models today.
Open modelWeights and training data, so you can audit or retrain it.
"Open source" in this bookAny model whose weights are public, whatever the data or licence.

📜 Three licence questions to always ask

1 · Commercial use allowed?

Meta's first Llama was non-commercial.

2 · Any limits?

Llama 2 and 3 need a special licence above 700 million monthly active users.

3 · Can outputs train other models?

Matters for synthetic data and distillation (a small "student" model copying a big "teacher"). Mistral originally banned it, later allowed it; Llama still didn't when the book was written.

⚠️ Common trap: data lineage Even if model X's licence allows training on its outputs, if X itself was trained on ChatGPT outputs against OpenAI's terms, using X may still be a problem. Know where a model's data came from.

Model API or self-host? Seven things to weigh

Build vs buy for models 📖 PDF p. 207–215

🧒 Plain English An inference service runs a model and answers requests; its interface is the model API. You can call a provider's API (OpenAI, Anthropic), a cloud's (Azure, GCP), a third party's hosted open model, or run a model yourself. The same model can behave slightly differently on different APIs, so re-test when you switch.
AxisUsing a model APISelf-hosting
🔒 Data privacyData leaves your company. Samsung banned ChatGPT after staff pasted in secrets; Zoom faced backlash over training on user dataData stays in-house
📜 Lineage & copyrightContracts may protect youFewer checks; if a model infringes, you're the likely target
🏆 PerformanceThe very best models are usually closedGap is closing, but the best open model will likely lag
🧰 FunctionalityScaling, function calling, structured output built in; logprobs often hidden; finetune only if allowedFull access to logprobs and internals; finetune freely (licence permitting)
💸 CostPay per token; can get very expensive at scaleEngineering talent and time to host, optimise, maintain
🎛️ ControlRate limits, silent model updates, over-cautious refusals, risk of losing accessFreeze a version; inspect changes; you maintain the API
📱 On-deviceNot possible without internetPossible, but hard
🧩 Real stories from the chapter
  • A 3D-character company kept getting "As an AI model, I don't have physical abilities" from commercial models, so it finetuned open models.
  • One study found StarCoder had memorised 8% of its training set. Memorised data can leak.
  • The same provider update made one company's intent classification 10% worse and another's support bot better.
🧪 Testing angle With an API, the model can change under you without notice. Keep a regression suite and run it on a schedule, not just when you change something. Prefer providers with a standard API (many copy OpenAI's) so swapping models is cheap.

Navigating public benchmarks and leaderboards

Useful for shortlisting, not for deciding 📖 PDF p. 215–221

🧒 Plain English There are thousands of benchmarks (Google's BIG-bench alone has 214). Evaluation harnesses run many at once: EleutherAI's supports 400+, OpenAI's evals ~500. A leaderboard picks some benchmarks and combines their scores to rank models. Two hard questions: which benchmarks, and how to combine them?

🤗 Hugging Face (late 2023)

Average of 6: ARC-C, MMLU, HellaSwag, TruthfulQA, WinoGrande, GSM-8K. Replaced in June 2024 with harder ones (MMLU-Pro, MATH lvl 5, GPQA, MuSR, BBH, IFEval).

🎓 Stanford HELM

10 benchmarks, only 2 shared with Hugging Face (MMLU, GSM-8K). Uses mean win rate instead of an average.

🔗 Correlated benchmarks double-count

Pair (from the book's table, Jan 2024)Correlation
MMLU ↔ WinoGrande0.90 · very strong
ARC-C ↔ WinoGrande0.89 · very strong
ARC-C ↔ MMLU0.87 · very strong
TruthfulQA ↔ ARC-C0.48 · moderate
HellaSwag ↔ GSM-8K0.35 · weak

Three reasoning benchmarks move together, so including all three over-weights reasoning. TruthfulQA moves on its own: getting better at reasoning and math doesn't automatically make a model more truthful.

⚠️ Common trap: simple averaging Averaging treats 80% on TruthfulQA the same as 80% on GSM-8K, even if one is far harder, and gives grade-school maths the same weight as truthfulness. For your own leaderboard, weight benchmarks by how much they matter to your app.
📌 Remember Running benchmarks isn't free: Stanford spent about $80,000–$100,000 evaluating 30 models on full HELM. Use public results to filter out bad models, then test a few finalists on your own data.

Data contamination

When a model has already seen the test 📖 PDF p. 221–224

🧒 Plain English Contamination (also called data leakage or "training on the test set") means a model was trained on the same data it's evaluated on, so it may just remember answers. A satirical paper trained a tiny 1-million-parameter model only on benchmark data and beat much larger models on those benchmarks.
🎯 Analogy A student who saw the exam paper beforehand. A high score doesn't mean they understand the subject. Passing bar exam questions doesn't make a model good at legal advice.

🕸️ Accidental

Web scraping picks up published benchmarks. As a friend of the author put it, a benchmark stops being useful once it's public.

📚 Same source

Training on maths textbooks that someone else also used to write a benchmark.

🎯 Deliberate (even for good reasons)

Training the final model on high-quality benchmark data to make it better for users.

🧩 Example OpenAI found 13 benchmarks with at least 40% of their data in GPT-3's training set. Good practice: say what share of a benchmark was in training, and give scores on clean items too. Benchmarks should keep a private hold-out part.
🧪 Testing angle Your private test set is your best defence: it can't be in anyone's training data if it was never published. Keep it private, and don't paste it into public tools.

Component-level and end-to-end checks

💬 Turn-based

Quality of each reply.

🎯 Task-based (more important)

Did the user's task get done, and in how many turns? Fixing a bug in 2 turns vs 20 is a big difference. Hard part: knowing where one task ends and the next begins.

🧩 A task-based benchmark: Twenty Questions One model instance thinks of a concept (e.g. apple); another asks yes/no questions to guess it. Score: did it guess, and in how many questions?
🎯 Analogy Unit tests per component plus an end-to-end test. Same idea as LLM Evaluation Ch 1's granularity ladder (span, step, turn, task).

Pipeline step 2: an evaluation guideline

The most important step 📖 PDF p. 226–227

🧒 Plain English The hardest part often isn't deciding whether an output is good, it's agreeing what "good" means. Define what the app should do and what it shouldn't (e.g. should a support bot discuss an upcoming election?). Ambiguous guidelines give ambiguous scores.
🧩 Correct isn't the same as good (LinkedIn) In a job-fit assessment feature, "You are a terrible fit" might be correct but isn't good. A good answer explains the gap between the job's requirements and the candidate's background, and how to close it.

This tells you whether improving the metric is worth investing in. Also set a usefulness threshold: e.g. below 50% factual consistency, the bot isn't usable at all.

Pipeline step 3: methods, data, slices, sample size

…and evaluating the evaluation itself 📖 PDF p. 228–232

🧰 Mix methods

Toxicity → small classifier; relevance → semantic similarity; factual consistency → AI judge. Or a cheap classifier on 100% of traffic plus an expensive judge on 1%.

📈 Use logprobs

Three classes at 30–40% each = the model isn't sure. One class at 95% = confident.

👩‍💼 Keep humans

Many teams use human review as their "North Star". LinkedIn manually reviewed up to 500 conversations a day.

📡 Plan for production

No reference answers there, but real users: plan what feedback to collect (Ch 10).

🍰 Slice your data

Look at results per subgroup: paying vs free users, mobile vs web, long vs short inputs. It reveals bias, helps debugging, and avoids Simpson's paradox:

Model AModel B
Group 193% (81/87) ✅87% (234/270)
Group 273% (192/263) ✅69% (55/80)
Overall78% (273/350)83% (289/350) 🤯

A wins in every group but loses overall, because the groups are different sizes for each model.

📌 Keep several evaluation sets One that mirrors real production traffic · one of known failure cases · one with user mistakes such as typos · an out-of-scope set the app should decline. The book's motto: "If you care about something, put a test set on it."

🔢 How many examples do you need?

Detect a 30% gap (~10)
Detect a 10% gap
~100
Detect a 3% gap
~1,000
Detect a 1% gap
~10,000 examples

OpenAI's rough guide for 95% confidence (a 30% gap needs only ~10). Rule of thumb: 3× smaller difference → 10× more examples. Benchmarks in EleutherAI's harness have a median of 1,000 examples; one prize's organisers called 300 the absolute minimum. (Bar lengths aren't to scale.)

⚙️ Algorithm: is my evaluation set big enough? (bootstrap, from the book) 
results ← []
repeat 20 times:
    sample ← pick_random(eval_set, n = len(eval_set), with_replacement)
    results.append(evaluate(model, sample))   # e.g. accuracy on this resample
if max(results) − min(results) is large:      # e.g. 90% on one, 70% on another
    print("Not trustworthy yet: add more examples")

🔍 Evaluate your evaluation pipeline

Right signals?

Do better answers really get higher scores? Do better scores lead to better business results?

Reliable?

Same run twice = same result? Set the AI judge's temperature to 0; keep configurations fixed.

Correlated metrics?

Perfectly correlated → drop one. Not correlated at all → an insight, or a broken metric.

Cost and latency?

Evaluation shouldn't make the app unusable. Skipping it to save time is a risky bet.

🧪 Testing angle Track every variable of each evaluation run (data, rubric, judge prompt, sampling settings), like experiment tracking. Change the pipeline when needs change, but not so often that results stop being comparable. (This matches the versioning advice in LLM Evaluation Ch 1.)
❓ Check yourself
A new prompt scores 3% better on 100 examples. Can you trust that?
Probably not. By OpenAI's rough guide, detecting a 3% difference with 95% confidence takes about 1,000 examples. Bootstrapping the 100 will likely show results moving by more than 3%.
Model A beats model B in every user segment but loses overall. What's going on?
Simpson's paradox: the segments have different sizes for each model, so the overall average is dominated by different groups. Look at the slices.

✅ One-page cheat sheet

  1. 🧪 Evaluation-driven development: write evaluation criteria before building, like TDD.
  2. 🧺 Criteria buckets: domain capability, generation quality, instruction following, cost and latency.
  3. ☑️ Multiple-choice tests check knowledge, not generation, and are sensitive to formatting.
  4. 🔍 Factual consistency: local (vs context) or global (vs world knowledge). Tools: AI judge, SelfCheckGPT, SAFE, entailment classifiers. Test niche topics and non-existent things.
  5. 🛡️ Safety: language, harmful advice, hate, violence, stereotypes, ideological bias. Cheap classifiers + judges.
  6. 📋 Instruction following: IFEval-style automatic checks; test your instructions. Roleplay: style + (negative) knowledge.
  7. ⚖️ Filter on must-haves first. Measure latency at P90/P95.
  8. 🧭 Model selection: filter hard attributes → shortlist with public data → test on your data → monitor.
  9. 📜 Open weight ≠ open model. Check licences: commercial use, limits, training on outputs.
  10. 🏠 API vs self-host: privacy, lineage, performance, functionality, cost, control, on-device.
  11. 📊 Leaderboards use few, often correlated benchmarks and naive averages. Use them to shortlist only.
  12. 🧫 Public benchmarks are often contaminated; detect with n-gram overlap or perplexity. Keep your test set private.
  13. 🧩 Pipeline: evaluate each component + end to end, turn and task.
  14. 📚 A clear guideline and rubric is the most important step. Tie metrics to business outcomes.
  15. 🍰 Slice results; beware Simpson's paradox. 3× smaller gap → 10× more examples. Bootstrap to check stability. Evaluate your evaluator.

This week's Saturday lab

Build and validate an AI judge

  1. Take your Week 3 golden dataset (30 cases). Write a rubric for one criterion (e.g. factual consistency of the summary, 0/1) with an example per score.
  2. Label 20 outputs yourself using the rubric.
  3. Write an AI-judge prompt from the rubric (Ch 3, topic 8), temperature 0, and score the same 20.
  4. Measure agreement between you and the judge. Read every disagreement: is the judge wrong, or your rubric unclear?
  5. Bootstrap your 30-case results 20 times. How much does the score move? Is 30 enough?
