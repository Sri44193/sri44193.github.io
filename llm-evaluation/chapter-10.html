The big picture

The whole chapter on one page. Come back here after each topic.

📌 The chapter in one sentence Don't approve a reasoning model on benchmark accuracy: measure final answers and the reasoning process separately, test depth and out-of-distribution generalisation, run repeated trials for stability, compare decoding policies, calibrate judges and verifiers, bound any self-correction loop, and define gates, fallbacks and human review.
🎯 The book's key analogy: two auditors Both approve the same insurance claim. One got there by luck, missing several policy violations; the other checked every clause and rule. Same answer, very different trustworthiness. Reasoning evaluation tries to tell them apart.

From fluency to reasoning: three phases

Plausible text isn't valid logic 📖 PDF p. 455–459

1 · Statistical fluencyNext-token predictors: great summaries and translations, but convincing prose can hide wrong or contradictory conclusions.
2 · Instruction followingSFT and RLHF make models follow tasks and intent, but they're not trained to verify every intermediate step.
3 · Reasoning-centricBreak problems into steps, explore alternatives, spend more compute on hard problems, use tools, reflect and revise.

🪞 Reflection

Review the first answer, find errors, revise (e.g. spot inconsistent ledger balances and recompute).

🛠️ Tool augmentation

Calculators, code interpreters, solvers, databases, policy engines for external verification. Many production systems mix neural reasoning with symbolic rules and checks.

🧩 Chatbot vs reasoning system (the book's analogy) A chatbot summarises customer emails. A reasoning system in insurance must check policy conditions, validate eligibility, detect contradictory evidence, cross-reference history, apply regulations and produce an auditable explanation. Errors there cost money and compliance.

An engineering definition of reasoning

Make it measurable 📖 PDF p. 459–461

🧒 The working definition Reasoning = producing logically coherent intermediate steps that transform the input into a goal-consistent output under constraints. A pragmatic choice, not a claim that models think like humans; visible traces may be incomplete or not faithful to what happened inside.

1️⃣ Step validity

Is each intermediate step valid?

2️⃣ State transitions

Does each step move correctly from one state to the next?

3️⃣ Goal consistency

Is it still heading for the goal?

4️⃣ Constraints

Are the rules satisfied?

5️⃣ Final output

Is the answer or action correct?

DeductiveApply rules to reach conclusionsFormal logic, theorem proving, rule-consistency checks
InductiveGeneralise from examplesPattern generalisation, unseen examples
AbductiveBest explanation for evidenceDiagnosis tasks, hypothesis ranking
AnalogicalMap structural similaritiesAnalogy completion

MathematicalSymbols and numbersGSM8K / MATH with step and symbolic checking
CommonsenseImplicit world knowledgeCounterfactual prompts, adversarial scenarios
Multi-hopCombine facts across stepsRetrieval-grounded multi-hop QA with evidence chains

Different models fail in different ways 📖 PDF p. 461–463

📌 Match tests to the model type Long-context → dependency tracking and lost-in-the-middle. Coding → execution, unit tests, repair loops. Multilingual → cross-language checks. Small models → latency, determinism, memory. MoE → routing consistency and collapse (Chapter 7). RL-trained → reward hacking, over-fitting to benchmark formats, fluent but invalid traces. The serving setup (API, local, edge) changes latency, reproducibility and observability too.
🧩 Reasoning SLMs at the edge A factory agent reads sensor logs, spots anomalous sequences, infers a likely machine fault, recommends a fix and triggers automation, all on local hardware. Evaluate response time, repeated-run consistency, device reliability, recovery and safe operation, not just answer quality.

5

Logical consistency and chain correctness in training

Reasoning hallucination 📖 PDF p. 468–470

🧒 Plain English Chain correctness = each step follows from the previous ones and contributes to the solution. In maths, check: variables stay consistently defined · equations stay algebraically valid · assumptions aren't contradicted later · number transformations are right.
⚠️ Reasoning hallucination Different from factual hallucination: the inference itself goes invalid while the language stays fluent. Example from the book: a model correctly finds ledger discrepancies but invents a causal link between unrelated events. Confident, plausible, wrong.
🧒 Chain-of-thought (CoT) supervision Train on the input, the reasoning chain and the answer, not just input → answer, so the trajectory itself is a learning target. Sources: synthetic traces, verified derivations, code execution paths, formal logic, multi-hop examples.
6

Process vs outcome supervision, reward hacking and faithfulness

Grade the working, not just the answer 📖 PDF p. 470–473

🎯 Outcome supervision

Checks only the final answer: "is the derivative right?"

🧭 Process supervision

Checks every step: were the differentiation rules and substitutions applied correctly? Tends to improve faithfulness, generalisation and robustness.

MethodWhat it does
Step verificationValidate each transition (humans, symbolic rules, execution, judge)
Process supervisionUse intermediate states in training or evaluation
Self-consistency checkingRun several times; do paths and answers converge?
Symbolic executionRun code, formulas or logic to verify the trace
Rule validationCheck domain rules, policies, maths transformations
Human annotationExperts score reasoning, find invalid steps
⚠️ Reward hacking RL can reward valid-looking reasoning (consistency, completeness, no contradictions, step validity). A sound derivation with a small slip may score higher than a lucky right answer. But models can learn traces that look valid to the reward model without reasoning better. Calibrate rewards against trusted references, execution, symbolic checks, experts and adversarial examples.
📌 Four signals, kept apart 1. Final-answer correctness · 2. Visible trace correctness · 3. Faithfulness of the trace to the real internal process · 4. Execution or tool verification. A model can pass 1 and 2 with a post-hoc rationalisation. Detect it with perturbation tests (small prompt changes should change reasoning appropriately), counterfactuals (change an assumption: does the conclusion update?), execution checks and internal probing where available. Black-box APIs: rely on repeated-run stability, contradiction rate, trace consistency and tool correctness.

Measuring reasoning depth

How many dependent steps before it breaks? 📖 PDF p. 473–476

🧒 Plain English Reasoning depth = how many (and how complex) dependent steps a model can complete before accuracy, consistency or constraint satisfaction starts to fall. Shallow: fact lookup, one-step sums. Deep: proofs, multi-hop science, debugging, legal compliance, workflow orchestration.
🧩 A seven-step audit chain (from the book) Parse transactions → detect anomalies → link entities across accounts → check regulations → infer causes → estimate risk → write an explainable report. A failure at any step propagates to the end.
⚙️ ➕ A depth curve you can build 
for difficulty in [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6] hops (or constraints, chain length, distractors):
    run 50 tasks at that difficulty
    record: accuracy, contradiction rate, step-validity score
plot each metric against difficulty
# "effective depth" = the point where accuracy or step validity drops below your threshold
🎯 Analogy A person doing a long proof forgets an early assumption and everything after it goes wrong. Transformers suffer similar context fragmentation when dependency tracking weakens over long chains.

Generalisation beyond memorisation

Change the surface, keep the problem 📖 PDF p. 476–479

🧒 Plain English A model that learned algebra templates may fail when you rename variables, reorder steps, add irrelevant distractors or make the notation more abstract. That reveals pattern matching, not reasoning. Test out-of-distribution, compositional, adversarial, cross-domain and abstract variants.
BenchmarkMeasuresMissesProduction caveat
Multi-hop QAChained inference over factsEvery step's validityAdd evidence-chain checks, retrieval grounding
GSM8KGrade-school arithmetic reasoningFlawed traces (answer-focused)Step verification, adversarial variants
MATHAdvanced mathsDomain workflowsSymbolic checks, solution path validation
GPQAGraduate-level scienceMixes knowledge and reasoningSeparate retrieval from reasoning validity
HumanEvalProgram synthesisLarge codebases, repair loopsUnit, integration and repository tasks
BIG-Bench HardCompositional stress testsProduction context and limitsCombine with domain test slices
📌 Remember Reasoning doesn't scale with parameter count alone: curriculum, supervision, specialisation, retrieval and test-time strategies matter, so smaller reasoning-tuned models sometimes beat bigger dense ones. Common deep-reasoning failures: drift, contradiction accumulation, premature conclusions, hallucinated logic, recursive error amplification, forgetting context. The real question isn't "does it truly reason?" but "is its reasoning valid, stable, verifiable and safe enough for this deployment?"
9

Step-level coherence and non-determinism

Same prompt, different reasoning 📖 PDF p. 479–485

🧒 Plain English Step-level coherence: each step stays consistent with the previous ones and useful for the goal. Reasoning drift: a wrong assumption at step 2 of a debugging task corrupts everything after, while the text still reads well. Non-determinism: the same prompt gives different reasoning, from sampling and from the serving system (hardware, batching, numerical kernels). In MoE models a slightly different prefix can activate different experts.

📏 Coherence metrics

Valid transition rate · contradictions per chain · assumption preservation · variable consistency · dependency resolution accuracy · step-to-goal relevance.

🔁 Stability methods

Repeated-run testing (answer agreement, trace similarity, contradictions) · trajectory alignment · symbolic or execution checks · tool-use validation · human or judge review.

🧩 The banking example One run flags a transaction as suspicious; another doesn't; both explanations sound plausible. Measure repeated-run agreement, classification flip rate, contradictions and trace similarity. If runs disagree on a high-risk decision: verify with tools, log both traces, block automated action, send to a human.
🧪 Testing angle ➕ Run every high-risk test case at least 5 times. Report the flip rate next to accuracy; a model that's 95% accurate but flips on 10% of borderline cases is not ready for automated decisions.

Decoding strategies and cascading reasoning failure

A small early error, a big final miss 📖 PDF p. 485–490

🎯 The book's analogy A spacecraft launched a fraction of a degree off ends up far from its orbit. A small decoding slip at step 3 of a proof corrupts every later step, even if the later steps are internally consistent: cascading reasoning failure.
StrategyGoodBad
Greedy (top token each step)Reproducible, stableRigid; stuck in a flawed path
Low temperatureMore deterministic, stableLess exploration; repetitive loops
High temperatureDiverse, explores moreLess coherent; more hallucination
Beam searchKeeps several paths, recovers locallyExpensive, slow; rarely used at scale
Self-consistencySample several chains, take the consistent answerCostly; a consistently wrong majority wins if all share a flaw; needs a selection rule or verifier
📌 Remember For high-risk decisions, self-consistency supports external verification; it doesn't replace it. Mitigations: reflection loops, verifier models, symbolic execution, iterative correction, tools. For code, execution feedback (compiler and runtime errors) is the strongest stabiliser.
🧩 The factory example An early decoding error infers a sensor fault → unnecessary maintenance is triggered → systems react to a false alarm → production disruption. Reasoning stability = operational reliability.
11

SFT + RL, and calibrating LLM-as-a-judge

Judges must be tested too 📖 PDF p. 490–494

📚 SFT

Teaches structured reasoning from good chain-of-thought examples. Risk: imitation without real correctness.

🏆 RL

Rewards reasoning quality, consistency and validity. DeepSeek-R1 also used RL to reduce language mixing and improve stability.

⚠️ Judges aren't reliable by default They may share the generator's blind spots, react to formatting, drift from the rubric, show bias, or be manipulated by judge-hacking text. Calibrate against trusted references, expert review and task rubrics.
🧩 The book's judge example Task: show that 1 + 3 + 5 + … + (2n − 1) = n². The generator writes a neat, well-formatted proof but uses the wrong expression for the nth odd number, so the summation logic is broken. A rubric-based verifier checks steps, variable consistency, symbolic transformations, continuity and completeness, finds the fatal error, and fails the proof, even though the statement itself is true.
➕ Check it yourself n = 4: 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16 = 4². The nth odd number is 2n − 1 (n = 4 → 7). A proof that uses, say, 2n + 1 would sum 3 + 5 + 7 + 9 = 24 ≠ 16, which is exactly the kind of step error a process check catches.

Bounded self-correction, and the full evaluation plan

Grading vs letting the student revise 📖 PDF p. 494–497

🎯 The book's analogy Pipeline 1: a teacher grades the exam and stops (good for benchmarks, reward modelling, datasets). Pipeline 2: the teacher gives feedback and the student revises (closer to modern reasoning systems; coding agents run code, read errors and fix).
📌 A repair loop is only safe with these guards Maximum repair attempts · an independent verifier or tool check · clear stopping rules · fallback escalation · logging of failed attempts · cost and latency limits · protection against rewriting the explanation without fixing the logic. The test isn't "is there feedback?" but "did feedback improve step validity, reduce contradictions, keep correct assumptions and give a verifiably better answer within budget?" High-impact domains still need experts, audit logs and human approval. 
evaluate a reasoning system (paraphrasing the book's checklist):
  final_answer_accuracy
  process_validity          # verify the steps with a verifier
  reasoning_depth           # accuracy by task depth
  generalization            # out-of-distribution and adversarial cases
  stability                 # repeated trials
  decoding_policy           # compare decoding settings
  judge_calibration         # verifier vs human review
  fallback_readiness        # escalation and human-review paths exist
❓ Check yourself
A model scores 92% on GSM8K. Why isn't that enough to deploy it for financial calculations?
It's answer-focused and may hide invalid reasoning; it doesn't test your domain, stability across runs, depth, distractors or tool use. Add step verification, repeated runs, domain slices and execution checks.
Self-consistency picks the majority answer from 7 chains. When can it still be wrong?
When most chains share the same flawed assumption: the majority is consistently wrong. Use an external verifier for high-risk cases.
Changing a key assumption in the prompt doesn't change the model's reasoning or conclusion. What might that reveal?
Possible unfaithful or post-hoc reasoning: the trace may not reflect how the answer was produced (counterfactual test failure).

✅ One-page cheat sheet

  1. 📈 Fluency → instruction following → reasoning-centric (decompose, explore, reflect, use tools).
  2. 🧭 Reasoning = coherent steps → goal-consistent output under constraints. Five checks: step validity, transitions, goal, constraints, final output.
  3. 🧩 Test each reasoning type (deductive, inductive, abductive, analogical, temporal, maths, commonsense, multi-hop) with its own method.
  4. 🏷️ Model families fail differently; SLMs need latency, determinism, memory tests.
  5. 🏗️ Each architecture piece has a risk: MoE routing, attention/lost-in-the-middle, RoPE position, reflection false-corrections, tool misuse.
  6. ⚠️ Reasoning hallucination: fluent but invalid inference.
  7. 🧭 Prefer process over outcome supervision; watch reward hacking.
  8. 🔍 Keep four signals apart: answer, visible trace, faithfulness, execution check. Use perturbation and counterfactual tests.
  9. 📊 Depth curve: accuracy and step validity vs hops, constraints, chain length, distractors.
  10. 🌐 Generalisation: rename, reorder, distract, abstract. Benchmarks have caveats.
  11. 🔁 Repeated runs: agreement, flip rate, trace similarity; disagreement on high risk → verify, block, escalate.
  12. 🎲 Decoding trade-offs; self-consistency can be consistently wrong; execution feedback stabilises code.
  13. ⚖️ Calibrate judges (blind spots, format bias, drift, judge hacking).
  14. 🛟 Self-correction must be bounded: attempt cap, independent verifier, stop rules, escalation, logs, budgets.

Try it: a mini lab

Test a reasoning model like this chapter suggests

  1. Pick 10 word problems. Make 3 variants of each: renamed variables, reordered facts, one irrelevant distractor. Compare accuracy.
  2. Run 5 problems 5 times each. Compute answer agreement and flip rate.
  3. For 3 answers, check every step by hand (or with a calculator). How many right answers had a wrong step?
  4. Use a second model as a judge with a step rubric. Compare its verdicts with yours (judge calibration).
  5. Change one assumption in a problem. Does the reasoning update correctly (counterfactual test)?
